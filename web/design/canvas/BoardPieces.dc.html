Pieces without the line
The ink casing had one job: keeping a seat colour apart from a tile colour. A line is the flattest way to do that. A shadow does it by saying the piece is above the tile, which is true, and which is also what the gaps between tiles are already saying about the whole island. So nothing on the board gets an outline. Tiles are separated by the ground showing through; pieces are separated by the light. A road is the gap itself painted in the seat colour, with the same shadow on one side.
The white seat is the test, and red on brick is the other one. Both pass on the shadow alone; on the hard shadow the edge is crisp, on the soft one it is quieter and the piece looks placed rather than printed. The plinth is the calmest but it costs the tile some colour under every piece.
Recommended: the soft shadow. Then motion does the rest of the separating. At rest a shadow breathes on a twelve-second cycle, barely. A piece you pick up hovers with its shadow left on the tile; a piece you place drops the last few pixels and the shadow snaps to it. Reduced motion keeps the shadows and drops the travel.
Roads shorten by nine pixels at each end so three roads meeting at a corner leave a chalk junction where a settlement lands. Today's rounded caps become square: a road is a segment of gap, not a pill. Red on brick is the one weak pair; if it bothers in play, the red seat moves a step toward crimson, not toward a line.
